Case study

Northgate Constructors.

How I built a construction WIP dashboard end to end — the scenario, the raw data, the model, and what it catches. Directed with AI in about 3 hours. Verified by me, line by line.

01 · The scenario

A contractor with $371M in flight —
and no early warning system.

Northgate Constructors is a fictional mid-size general contractor: eight active projects — water treatment, hospitals, wind farms, gas processing — worth $371M in revised contract value. Fictional, because my professional work is confidential. The mechanics, however, are exactly how construction finance works.

Construction recognizes revenue on percentage of completion: if you've incurred 60% of a job's expected cost, you've earned 60% of its contract value — no matter what you've actually invoiced. That gap between earned and billed is where the danger hides. Bill behind your earnings and you're quietly financing your client. Let a margin slip month over month and you won't see it in the GL until quarter-end — when it's already a loss.

A controller's defense is the WIP schedule. Most live in spreadsheets nobody opens. I wanted one that surfaces its own problems.

02 · The data

Raw inputs, built to behave
like the real thing.

Generated programmatically so it carries the texture of real project data: 384 monthly cost rows across labour, materials, subcontracts, and equipment; twelve months of progress billings; and a change-order register where — as auditors insist — only approved COs revise contract value. Pending ones are tracked, but kept out.

The portfolio: eight projects, revised contract values, current cost estimates — all formulas, nothing hardcoded.

A slice of the cost detail feeding the model, month by month, category by category.

03 · The model

Fifteen measures.
Four ideas carry the weight.

The DAX was drafted with AI assistance and verified by me against source data — every measure, every total. These four do the heavy lifting:

% Complete

Cost to Date ÷ Estimated Total Cost

The cost-to-cost method — the standard way construction measures progress under percentage-of-completion accounting.

Earned Revenue

SUMX per project: % Complete × Revised Contract

Computed project by project, then summed — an aggregate ratio would blend projects together and quietly get it wrong.

Over/(Under) Billing

Billed to Date − Earned Revenue

Overbilled is a liability you owe in work. Underbilled is your cash sitting in the client's account. Both are normal — in moderation.

Revised Contract Value

Original + APPROVED change orders only

Counting pending COs in contract value is one of the most common construction audit findings. The model refuses by design.

04 · The report

Two pages. Every problem
on the first screen.

An executive overview — KPI row, full WIP schedule with rule-based flags, billing position, margin vs threshold — and a project deep dive with cumulative S-curves, cost mix, and the change-order register. Click to enlarge.

Margin fade, caught early

Larkspur Transit Garage is 88.5% complete earning 2.0% — roughly $425K of profit on a $21M job, one bad month from break-even. Flagged red on the schedule, with no change-order upside left to rescue it.

Silent underbilling

Riverbend Water Treatment sits $8.8M behind its earned revenue — work performed, cash unclaimed. The overview shows the whole book is net $17.9M underbilled: an interest-free loan to clients, visible at a glance.

Change-order exposure

Coyote Flats carries $3.2M of pending change orders — outside contract value until approved, exactly where auditors expect them, but tracked so the earned-revenue swing is no surprise when they land.

05 · The build

Three directed hours.
A twenty-hour job by hand.

This is the part that matters most to me. I didn't hand-craft this dashboard, and I didn't just prompt for it either. I directed it: specified the model and the accounting rules, had AI generate the dataset and draft the DAX, then verified every measure against source before it shipped. The same loop I run at work with Copilot — and the same loop I'd bring to any team adopting AI seriously.
